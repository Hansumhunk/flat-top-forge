How to choose a flat-top griddle

Do not start with the logo on the lid. Start with how many people you cook for, how many foods need different temperatures at the same time, where the griddle will live, and how much maintenance you want. Then compare brands inside the class that actually fits you.

The buying order
  1. Choose the size class. Decide how much food you want cooking at once and how much space you can give the appliance.
  2. Choose the heat-control layout. One, two, three, or four independently controlled burners or electric zones can matter as much as width.
  3. Choose gas or electric. Location, power availability, indoor-use rating, surface material, and cleanup all change with the fuel system.
  4. Choose the cooking surface. Bare seasoned steel and coated/nonstick surfaces have different tool and maintenance requirements.
  5. Check the ownership details. Hood, shelves, grease system, storage, mobility, warranty, accessory support, and exact dimensions decide whether the machine is pleasant after the excitement of buying it wears off.

1. Start with size, but do not stop there

Size classUsually makes sense forMain tradeoff
17–22 inch1–3 people, camping, tailgating, small patios, simple mealsEasy to move and store, but limited room for simultaneous foods
28 inch2–5 people, everyday family cooking, moderate multi-item mealsStrong middle ground, but large breakfasts may still require batches
30–32 inch4–7 people, mixed meals, cooks who want more working room without the largest cartMore flexibility, less portability
36 inch and larger5+ people, entertaining, large breakfasts, hibachi-style meals, high-volume cooksMaximum workspace with the biggest patio, storage, fuel, and movement commitment

Nominal width is only a shorthand. Compare the actual cooking-area dimensions and square inches for the model you are considering.

2. Burner count and heat zones are a separate buying decision

Two griddles can be similar in width and still cook very differently because one may have two independently controlled burners while another has three or four. More controls give you more ways to divide the surface into different jobs at the same time.

Independent zonesTypical workflowBest fit
1 zoneOne general surface temperatureCompact electric units, simple breakfast foods, sandwiches, basic batch cooking
2 zonesHot side + medium/cool sideBurgers plus buns, steak plus vegetables, sear + finish, ordinary family meals
3 zonesHot + medium + low/holdingFull breakfasts, fajitas, fried rice, proteins with sides, more controlled multi-item timing
4 zonesMultiple sear/cook/finish/hold areasLarge breakfasts, hibachi-style cooking, parties, several proteins and sides at once

Burner count is not the same as four perfectly isolated rectangles. Steel spreads heat sideways, food load pulls heat from the plate, wind affects gas appliances, and burner geometry varies. Think of extra burners or elements as more control authority, not invisible walls between temperatures.

How many zones should you actually buy?

Simple cook

1–2 zones

Enough if you mostly make burgers, sandwiches, pancakes, bacon, or one protein with one side and do not mind moving food in batches.

Flexible family cook

2–3 zones

A useful sweet spot for families who cook protein, vegetables, buns, potatoes, or breakfast components at different temperatures.

Complex cook

3–4 zones

Worth considering if you routinely run a sear zone, medium cooking zone, gentle zone, and holding area at the same time.

High-volume cook

4 zones + large surface

Best suited to crowds, hibachi-style meals, large breakfasts, meal prep, and cooks who hate stopping to clear the griddle between components.

A model's number of burners or electric control zones must be checked on the current manufacturer specification page. Do not assume every 28-inch or 36-inch griddle uses the same layout.

3. Gas vs electric changes more than the power source

Gas usually gives you the broadest selection of large outdoor griddles and lets you cook away from an electrical outlet. Electric can provide thermostat-style control, indoor-rated countertop options, and coated surfaces that simplify some cleanup. Larger multi-zone electric flat tops now exist too, so electric should not automatically be treated as the small-appliance category.

The important buying questions are: Where will you cook? Is indoor use required? Do you have the needed electrical circuit or gas supply? Do you want seasoned steel or a coated surface? How many independently controlled zones do you need?

6. The boring features become important after week one

  • Hood or hard cover: useful for protection and storage, but designs differ in whether they are meant to stay closed during cooking.
  • Side shelves: prep space can matter almost as much as cooking space during large meals.
  • Grease management: check where the grease cup or tray sits, how easy it is to remove, and whether liners are proprietary.
  • Mobility: casters, folding legs, lift-off cooktops, handles, and total weight matter if the griddle moves often.
  • Storage footprint: measure the actual assembled dimensions with shelves, hood, and handles, not merely the nominal cooktop width.
  • Fuel / power: confirm propane tank arrangement, natural-gas compatibility, voltage, wattage, plug type, circuit needs, and extension-cord restrictions.
  • Warranty: compare coverage length and what parts are actually included.
  • Accessory ecosystem: covers, shelves, wind guards, tool racks, replacement tops, grease liners, and model-specific accessories can change long-term usefulness.

7. Use this shortlist before comparing brands

  1. How many people do I normally cook for?
  2. Do I want to cook everything at once or am I fine with batches?
  3. How many different temperatures do I realistically need at one time?
  4. Do I need 1, 2, 3, or 4 independently controlled zones?
  5. Will this be indoor, outdoor, portable, built-in, or mostly stationary?
  6. Do I want gas, electric, or am I open to either?
  7. Do I want seasoned steel or a coated surface?
  8. How much patio, counter, and storage space do I actually have?
  9. What hood, shelf, grease, wheel, and prep-space features matter to me?
  10. What accessories and replacement parts are available for the exact model?
  11. What does the warranty cover?
  12. What is my real total budget after cover, tools, fuel/power needs, and accessories?
Buying-guide rule: size class, burner count, and zone count should narrow the field before brand preference. A four-burner 36-inch griddle and a two-burner 36-inch griddle may share a width but offer very different control during a complex cook.
