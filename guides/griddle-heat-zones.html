How to use heat zones on a griddle

A flat top gets much easier when you stop treating the whole cooktop like one giant frying pan and start using it like several temperature stations sharing the same slab of steel.

The basic pattern

Hot zone

Use for hard searing, smash burgers, browning steak, or anything that benefits from fast color.

Medium zone

Use for vegetables, chicken, eggs, pancakes, reheating, and foods that need more controlled cooking.

Holding zone

Use lower heat or an unlit area to park cooked food briefly while the rest of the meal catches up.

Why zones work

Steel stores and spreads heat, but the surface is rarely identical from corner to corner. Burner layout, wind, food load, and preheat time all affect temperature. Deliberately creating zones gives you somewhere to move food when it is browning too quickly or waiting on another component.

A simple two-burner setup

Run one side hotter for searing and the other side lower for cooking through or holding. On a larger multi-burner griddle, you can create a hotter end, a medium center, and a lower finishing zone.

Preheat before judging the surface

Give the griddle time to stabilize before deciding one side is weak or uneven. Loading cold food onto the surface will temporarily pull heat out of the steel. That temperature drop is normal and becomes easier to manage when you have more than one zone available.

Use an infrared thermometer as a reference, not a religion

An infrared thermometer can help you learn where the surface runs hotter or cooler. Treat it as one tool alongside visual browning, food response, burner settings, and experience. Different surface conditions can influence readings.
